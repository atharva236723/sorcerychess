Sorcery Chess strategy guide

Sorcery Chess rewards the same deep thinking as ordinary chess, but the bigger board and the four spellcasters open up tactics you will not find anywhere else. This guide covers the ideas that win games — how to open, how to use each spell, how to manage the clock, how to keep your king safe, and how to close out an endgame. If you have not learned the rules yet, start with our how-to-play guide first.

Opening principles on a 12×12 board

The classic opening principles still apply, just on a grander scale. Fight for the centre of the board, because pieces in the middle control more squares. Develop your knights, bishops and spellcasters toward active squares instead of moving the same piece twice. And keep your king safe — with no castling in Sorcery Chess, you tuck the king away by walking it a couple of squares toward a corner behind a wall of pawns, rather than castling in one move. Because the board is larger, development takes a few more moves than in standard chess, so avoid grabbing material early if it means leaving your back ranks undeveloped.

Using the Wizard: poison with purpose

The Wizard is your sharpest attacking tool, but it only has four casts and needs two moves to recharge, so do not waste poison on a pawn. Save it for a high-value target — an enemy queen, a rook, or an opposing spellcaster — ideally one that is defended, since poison kills it without you having to trade a piece for it. Remember that casting also swaps the Wizard with its target, so check where the Wizard lands: you can use the swap to leap the Wizard to safety, or you can accidentally drop it onto a dangerous square. A poisoned piece still moves and captures for two more moves before it dies, so poison a piece that is stuck defending something rather than one that can run in and do damage first.

Using the Freezer: stop threats cold

The Freezer never captures, so think of it as control rather than force. Its best use is defensive: freezing the enemy piece that is giving check is a completely legal way to escape check, and freezing a queen or rook for three moves can defuse an entire attack while you reorganise. Offensively, freeze a key defender for three moves and pour your own pieces through the gap it leaves behind. Like the Wizard, the Freezer swaps places with its target, so use that swap to reposition it — but never leave the Freezer somewhere it can be captured for free, because it cannot fight back.

Using the Alchemist: your insurance policy

The Alchemist has unlimited uses and one job: cure your poisoned pieces before they die. If your opponent has a Wizard, keep the Alchemist developed and within reach of your valuable pieces so a poisoned queen or rook can be saved in time. Even the threat of a cure changes your opponent's maths — if they know you can undo their poison, they may hold the spell rather than spend it. Because the Alchemist also swaps places with the ally it cures, it doubles as a way to reposition an important piece to safety.

Using the Witch: the endgame trump card

The Witch raises a fallen piece from your graveyard and dies in its place, so it is a sacrifice — spend it when the piece you get back is worth more than the Witch, or when you desperately need firepower. The classic play is to lose a queen early, then, at a critical moment, sacrifice the Witch to bring that queen back onto the board and swing the game. Because no captured piece is ever truly gone, the endgame in Sorcery Chess stays dangerous far longer than in ordinary chess — never assume the position is settled while an enemy Witch still stands.

Managing the clock

In a timed game you have five minutes and a two-second increment per move, so tempo matters. Do not burn three minutes on the opening; play your development quickly and save your thinking time for the sharp middlegame, where a single spell can decide the game. If your opponent is low on time, keep the position complicated and force them to calculate — many games are won on flag fall rather than checkmate. Against the two dread AI opponents the clock is only three minutes a side, so speed matters even more.

King safety on the open board

A 12×12 board gives attacking pieces long, open lines, so an exposed king is even more dangerous here than in standard chess. Get your king to safety early, keep a few pawns in front of it, and be especially wary of enemy spellcasters: a Freezer can lock down the piece defending your king, and a Wizard can poison a key defender from a distance. When you attack the enemy king, combine ordinary pieces with a spell — for example, freeze a defender, then bring your queen and rook to the newly weakened square.

Climbing the difficulty ladder

Start against the Apprentice to get comfortable, then step up to the Sorcerer and Archmage as your play sharpens. The two hardest opponents, The Malevolent and The Dominion, search several moves ahead and hunt your king aggressively — beat the levels before them to unlock the challenge. If you want to drill pure fundamentals without magic, switch to Classic mode for a traditional 8×8 game; the tactics you learn there carry straight back into Sorcery Chess.

Keep improving

The single best way to get better is to play often and pay attention to what each spell does to a position. Study the four pieces in depth in The Grimoire, review the exact scoring on the Points & Ranks page, and put it all into practice.
